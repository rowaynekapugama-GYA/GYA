The way to get more Google reviews without breaking AHPRA rules is to ask every patient for honest feedback, in the same way, as part of your normal routine, and then leave those reviews where they are. Don't offer anything in return, don't only ask the happy ones, don't write or edit reviews yourself, and don't copy reviews about clinical care into your website, ads or social posts. Do that, reply to every review politely without sharing any patient details, and the reviews build up on their own over a few months.

That's the short version. Here's how it works in practice.

## Why reviews matter so much for a dental practice

Reviews do two jobs. Google uses them when it decides which practices to show in the map results, and patients read them before they pick up the phone. A practice with a steady trickle of recent reviews looks open, busy and looked after. One with twelve reviews, the newest from two years ago, looks like it might have closed.

It's one of the pieces we work on when we're moving a practice up the map, alongside the listing itself and the website. I went through the full order in [how to rank a dental practice on Google Maps](how-to-rank-a-dental-practice-on-google-maps.html).

## Where the AHPRA line actually sits

This is the bit that makes practice owners nervous, so let's make it simple.

A review a patient leaves on your Google listing, in their own words, isn't something you wrote or paid for. That's very different from you taking that review and putting it in front of people to sell your services.

The problem starts when a review becomes part of your advertising. AHPRA doesn't allow testimonials about clinical care in advertising for a regulated health service. So a Google review that says "Dr Smith fixed my tooth and it doesn't hurt anymore" is fine sitting on Google. Screenshot it onto your Instagram, drop it on your implants page, or quote it in a Google ad, and now it's a clinical testimonial in your advertising.

Reviews about the non-clinical side, like how friendly the front desk was or how easy parking is, are a different story. But the safest habit for most practices is just to leave reviews on Google and not reuse them anywhere.

## How to ask without crossing the line

**Ask everyone, the same way.** Make it part of the routine, not a special favour you ask of patients who seemed thrilled. A line at the desk or a text after the appointment that says something like "We'd love to hear how we went, here's the link" works well. Google's own rules also say you can't pick and choose who you ask based on whether you think they'll be positive.

**Keep the wording neutral.** "Tell us how we went" is fine. "Leave us a 5 star review" isn't, and it reads as pushy anyway.

**Never offer anything in return.** No discount, no free whitening, no entry into a prize draw. Paying for reviews in any form is against Google's policies, and it makes every review on your listing look less believable. It's not worth it.

**Don't write reviews yourself.** Not you, not your staff, not your partner, not a friend of the practice. It's also the kind of thing that gets noticed, and a listing that loses a batch of reviews overnight looks worse than one that never had them.

**Make the link easy.** Most patients are happy to leave a review if it takes thirty seconds. A short link that goes straight to the review box on your listing makes a big difference. If they have to search for you, find the right listing and scroll down, most won't bother.

## How to reply to reviews

Reply to every review, good or bad. It shows patients reading later that a real person is paying attention.

The rule with replies is privacy. Don't confirm that someone is a patient, don't mention their treatment, and don't argue the details in public, even if the review is unfair. Something like "Thanks for taking the time to leave feedback. We'd really like to talk this through, please give us a call on the practice number" is enough. Then have the actual conversation off Google.

For the good ones, a simple thank you is plenty. Avoid replies that turn into a sales pitch for a treatment, since your replies are your words, not the patient's.

## What about a bad review?

Every practice gets one eventually. If it breaks Google's policies, like it's clearly fake or abusive, you can flag it to Google for removal. If it's just a patient who had a bad day with you, reply calmly using the privacy rules above and fix whatever went wrong. A handful of honest, mixed reviews with thoughtful replies reads as more believable to patients than a wall of perfect fives.
