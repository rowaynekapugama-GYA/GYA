Every dental Google Ads account needs negative keywords for six kinds of search: people looking for dental jobs, people studying dentistry, people trying to fix their teeth at home, people hunting for something free, treatments you don't offer, and places you don't serve. Words like "jobs", "salary", "course", "DIY", "at home" and "free" stop you paying for clicks from people who were never going to book. Add them before the first ad goes live, then check your search terms report regularly and keep adding to the list.

That's the short version. Here's why it matters, and the lists we start every dental account with.

## Why dental accounts leak money

When you bid on a word like "dentist", Google doesn't only show your ad to people who want a dentist. It shows it to anyone whose search looks close enough. "Dentist jobs Sydney." "How much do dentists earn." "Dentist that does payment plans for free." Each of those clicks costs you the same as a real patient's click.

Nobody does this on purpose. Most accounts I look at for the first time were set up with good keywords and no negatives at all, or a short list someone added once and never touched again. The ads are fine. The money is just going to the wrong people.

Negative keywords are the fix. They tell Google: if a search has this word in it, don't show my ad.

## The six lists we start with

These are the groups we build on day one. Treat them as a starting point, not a finished list. Your practice will have its own.

### 1. Jobs and careers

This is the biggest one in most accounts. Dental assistants, students and dentists all search for work, and their searches look a lot like patient searches.

Start with: jobs, job, career, careers, hiring, vacancy, vacancies, salary, wage, pay rate, resume, recruitment, employment, internship, seek.

### 2. Study and training

People researching how to become a dentist, hygienist or dental nurse.

Start with: course, courses, degree, university, uni, TAFE, study, studying, training, certificate, qualification, school, exam.

### 3. DIY and home remedies

Someone searching "how to whiten teeth at home" or "DIY retainer" isn't looking to book. Be a little careful here, because some "how to" searches do come from patients. Check your own search terms before you block "how" on its own.

Start with: DIY, at home, home remedy, natural remedy, kit, homemade, YouTube, video.

### 4. Free and bargain hunting

This one depends on your practice. If you don't bulk bill and don't run any free offers, add: free, bulk billed, bulk billing, government, public dental, voucher, coupon. If you do offer any of these, leave the matching word out, and make sure the offer on your landing page has clear terms. That's an AHPRA requirement, not just good manners.

### 5. Treatments you don't offer

If you don't do braces, sleep apnoea devices or general anaesthetic, every click from those searches is wasted. Go through your treatment list with the team and add anything you don't provide, plus any brands you don't stock.

The same goes for adjacent professions that share words with dentistry: denture clinic, prosthetist, vet, veterinary, dog, cat. Yes, people search "dog teeth cleaning" and your "teeth cleaning" ad can turn up.

### 6. Places you don't serve

Patients book close to home. Location targeting does most of the work, but people often type a suburb or city into the search itself. If you're in Crows Nest, someone searching "dentist Penrith" is unlikely to make the drive. Add the suburbs and cities far enough away that you'd never see those patients in the chair.

## Keep it working after day one

A negative keyword list isn't a set and forget job. Three habits keep it useful.

**Read the search terms report.** In Google Ads this shows the actual words people typed before they clicked. Look at it regularly. Every account throws up searches you'd never have guessed, and each one is a new negative.

**Watch the plurals and spellings.** Negative keywords don't stretch to cover close variations the way normal keywords do. Blocking "job" won't always block "jobs". Add both.

**Don't over-block.** It's easy to get carried away and knock out real patients. "Cheap" is a good example. Some practices block it, others find people searching "cheap dentist" book check-ups and stay for years. Decide based on what your bookings tell you, not on how the word sounds.

## Why one list isn't enough

Here's something we do differently. We build every dental account [per treatment and per suburb](../dental-google-ads.html), with each treatment in its own campaign. That means negative keywords work at two levels.

There's the shared list above, which applies everywhere. Then each campaign gets its own negatives so the campaigns don't trip over each other. The implant campaign blocks "check-up" and "clean". The check-up campaign blocks "implant". So a patient searching for implants sees the implant ad and lands on the implant page, and a cheap check-up search can't quietly use up the money meant for implants.

We build those lists before the first ad runs, and every ad and landing page is checked against the AHPRA advertising guidelines before it goes live too. Then we look at the search terms with you on our fortnightly call, alongside the thing that matters most: how many of those clicks turned into booked patients.
